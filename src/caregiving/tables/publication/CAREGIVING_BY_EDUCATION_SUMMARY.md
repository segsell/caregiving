# Caregiving Arrangement Responses by Education Level

Summary of Panels B (overall), C (low education), and D (high education) from `policy_behavioral_changes.tex`. All shares are conditional on care demand > 0.

## 1. Headline: High-education agents respond far more strongly

| Metric (All 40--67) | Baseline | % chg Normal | % chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overall** | | | |
| Informal CG | 42.6% | +8.0% | +9.9% |
| Formal care | 33.4% | -10.2% | -12.6% |
| **Low Education** | | | |
| Informal CG | 42.9% | +6.6% | +7.3% |
| Formal care | 33.6% | -8.4% | -9.4% |
| **High Education** | | | |
| Informal CG | 42.2% | +11.4% | +15.7% |
| Formal care | 33.0% | -14.5% | -20.0% |

The leave policy increases informal caregiving overall by 8--10%. But this aggregate masks a large education gradient: **high-edu agents shift toward informal care almost twice as much as low-edu agents** (+11.4% vs +6.6% under normal leave; +15.7% vs +7.3% under full leave).

## 2. Baseline differences in care composition

- **Low education**: higher baseline intensive CG (21.6% vs 12.6% high), lower light CG (21.2% vs 29.6% high). Low-edu agents already bear the heavier caregiving burden in the baseline.
- **High education**: more light care (29.6%), less intensive care (12.6%). More formal care usage is similar across groups (~33%).
- Both groups have similar overall informal CG rates in the baseline (~42--43%), but the *composition* of informal care differs starkly.

## 3. Age gradient differs by education

The strongest policy responses occur in the 50--59 age range for both groups, but the magnitude differs sharply:

| Age group | Low Edu: Informal CG %chg (Normal/Full) | High Edu: Informal CG %chg (Normal/Full) |
|---|---|---|
| 40--49 | +4.6% / +5.3% | +4.1% / +7.1% |
| 50--59 | +9.7% / +10.6% | +17.0% / +21.7% |
| 55--59 (peak) | +10.6% / +11.6% | **+20.3% / +25.7%** |
| 60--67 | +3.5% / +4.1% | +8.2% / +12.7% |

For high-edu agents aged 55--59, informal care rises by over 20% under normal leave and 26% under full leave. The same age group for low-edu agents shows only about half that response. This is the age range where wages (and therefore opportunity costs) peak for high earners.

## 4. Formal care crowding-out is much larger for high education

| Age group | Low Edu: Formal care %chg (Normal/Full) | High Edu: Formal care %chg (Normal/Full) |
|---|---|---|
| 50--59 | -12.1% / -13.3% | **-19.8% / -25.3%** |
| 55--59 (peak) | -13.8% / -15.1% | **-22.2% / -28.1%** |
| All (40--67) | -8.4% / -9.4% | **-14.5% / -20.0%** |

High-edu agents reduce formal care usage roughly twice as much as low-edu agents. Under full leave in the 55--59 group, formal care drops by 28% for high-edu vs 15% for low-edu.

## 5. Normal vs. full leave: high-edu much more sensitive to generosity

The gap between normal leave (65% replacement) and full leave (100% replacement) reveals how sensitive each group is to the degree of wage replacement:

- **Low education**: full leave barely adds anything beyond normal (informal CG: +7.3% vs +6.6%, a 0.7pp additional effect).
- **High education**: full leave is substantially more powerful (informal CG: +15.7% vs +11.4%, a 4.3pp additional effect).

This is consistent with the mechanism: higher wages mean the difference between 65% and 100% replacement is larger in absolute terms for high-edu agents, providing a stronger marginal incentive.

## 6. Intensive care: largest *relative* response is among high-edu

| Metric (All 40--67) | Baseline Low | Baseline High | %chg Normal Low | %chg Normal High |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intensive CG | 21.6% | 12.6% | +7.8% | **+14.8%** |
| Light CG | 21.2% | 29.6% | +5.3% | +10.0% |

High-edu agents have low baseline intensive care (12.6%) but show a 15% increase under normal leave and 21% under full leave. The policy induces them to take on intensive caregiving roles they would not have chosen in the baseline. For low-edu agents, who already provide intensive care at higher rates, the relative response is more muted.

## 7. Economic interpretation

The pattern is consistent with the **opportunity cost mechanism**:

1. High-edu agents have higher wages, so the baseline cost of giving up work for caregiving is higher. They opt for formal care instead.
2. The leave policy replaces a share of prior wages, reducing the opportunity cost. Because high-edu wages are higher, the absolute reduction in opportunity cost is larger.
3. This causes a disproportionate shift from formal to informal care among high-edu agents.
4. The full leave (100% replacement) amplifies this effect more for high-edu agents because the absolute gap between 65% and 100% replacement is larger.
5. Low-edu agents already provide more informal (especially intensive) care in the baseline — there is less behavioral margin to shift.

**Policy implication**: The leave policy's primary behavioral effect operates through high-education agents who substitute away from formal care. The fiscal cost of the policy is therefore concentrated on subsidizing high earners' informal care provision, while low-edu agents — who already bear the heavier caregiving burden — see comparatively smaller behavioral changes.

# Labor Supply Dynamics of Caregivers Under Leave Policies

Summary of Panels E--M from `policy_behavioral_changes.tex`. Covers labor composition of current and ever-caregivers, the first caregiving year, post-CG labor supply, and heterogeneity by caregiving duration.

## 8. During active caregiving: massive labor supply reduction (Panel E)

The most dramatic behavioral response is among **current** informal caregivers:

| Metric (All 40--67) | Baseline | Normal leave | Full leave | %chg Normal | %chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Employment rate among CG | 57.9% | 49.4% | 48.6% | **-14.8%** | **-16.1%** |
| Share FT among CG | 26.3% | 21.9% | 20.4% | -16.6% | -22.5% |
| Share PT among CG | 31.7% | 27.5% | 28.3% | -13.2% | -10.8% |
| Share unemp. among CG | 28.8% | 39.8% | 41.0% | **+38.5%** | **+42.5%** |
| Share retired among CG | 13.3% | 10.8% | 10.4% | -18.8% | -21.7% |

The leave policy causes a **15--16% drop in employment** among active caregivers and a corresponding **38--43% surge in unemployment** (leave take-up). Both FT and PT work decline, but the shift is sharpest for full-time workers. The "unemployment" category here reflects agents who stop working to provide care while receiving the leave benefit.

### Age pattern within current caregivers

The response is strongest in the **50--59** age range:

| Age group | Empl. rate %chg (Normal/Full) | Unemp. %chg (Normal/Full) |
|---|---|---|
| 40--49 | -8.4% / -8.6% | +23.3% / +23.8% |
| 50--59 | **-21.1% / -22.6%** | **+52.7% / +56.4%** |
| 55--59 (peak) | **-23.4% / -25.3%** | **+51.5% / +55.8%** |
| 60--67 | -5.8% / -8.0% | +26.5% / +32.6% |

At ages 50--59, more than half of the increase in unemployment among caregivers is attributable to the leave policy. At 60--67 the effect is more muted, partly because the retirement channel also absorbs agents in this range.

### Retirement among caregivers drops

Share retired among caregivers at 60--67 falls from 37.9% to 31.8% (-16.3% normal) / 30.7% (-19.2% full). The leave policy **keeps older caregivers attached to the labor market** — they take leave rather than retiring early to provide care.

## 9. Lifetime labor supply of ever-caregivers: virtually unchanged (Panels F, L)

Despite the dramatic within-caregiving-episode effects, the **overall lifetime** labor supply of ever-caregivers barely moves:

| Metric (All 30--67) | Panel F (all periods) | Panel L (excl. 1st CG yr) |
|---|---|---|
| Employment rate baseline | 67.1% | 67.0% |
| Employment rate %chg Normal | +0.05% | +0.18% |
| Employment rate %chg Full | +0.06% | +0.19% |

The leave policy mainly **redistributes when agents work**, not whether they work over the lifecycle. The small positive effect on lifetime employment suggests the job retention component slightly increases total career employment.

### Lifecycle redistribution pattern

- **Ages 30--49**: Tiny positive employment effects (+0.1--0.3%). Pre-caregiving labor supply is barely affected.
- **Ages 50--59**: Small negative effects (-1.8% to -2.4%). This is the peak caregiving window where agents reduce work temporarily.
- **Ages 60--67**: Clear positive effects (+4.2% employment rate for ever-CG). Agents who would have retired to provide care instead take leave and **return to work** afterward. Retirement share at 60--67 drops by 3.7--4.2%.

The offsetting positive effect at older ages approximately balances the negative effect during the CG window, producing near-zero net lifetime effects.

## 10. The first caregiving year: a point-in-time disruption (Panel K)

At the moment agents first begin caregiving:

| Metric (All 30--67) | Baseline | %chg Normal | %chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employment rate | 72.7% | **-4.2%** | **-4.3%** |
| Share FT | 32.4% | -6.2% | **-11.1%** |
| Share PT | 40.3% | -2.6% | +1.1% |

The leave policy reduces employment by about 4% at the onset of caregiving. The FT decline is much larger under full leave (-11.1% vs -6.2%), while PT actually *rises* under full leave (+1.1%). Under full (100%) wage replacement, agents shift from full-time to part-time rather than stopping work entirely.

The 30--39 age bins are all NaN because caregiving demand does not arise before age 40 in the model.

### First CG year: sharpest effects at older ages

| Age group | FT %chg (Normal/Full) | Empl. rate %chg (Normal/Full) |
|---|---|---|
| 40--49 | -4.7% / -9.0% | -2.5% / -2.4% |
| 55--59 | **-9.0% / -15.1%** | -7.0% / -7.6% |
| 60--67 | -8.7% / -15.0% | -6.6% / -8.1% |

Agents who begin caregiving later in life (55+) reduce FT work by 9--15%. At 60--67, the employment rate effect reaches -6.6 to -8.1%, consistent with these agents being on the margin between working and retiring.

## 11. Duration of caregiving determines long-run impact (Panel M)

The most nuanced finding: the policy's effects differ starkly by how long agents ultimately provide care.

### 1 CG year: barely affected

| Metric (All 30--67) | Baseline | %chg Normal | %chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employment rate | 68.8% | -0.3% | -0.4% |
| Share retired (63--67) | 65.0% | +0.4% | +0.5% |

Short-duration caregivers see negligible lifetime labor changes. Their one-year caregiving episode is too brief to generate meaningful behavioral responses.

### 2--3 CG years: small effects

| Metric (All 30--67) | Baseline | %chg Normal | %chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employment rate | 67.5% | -0.06% | -0.04% |
| Share retired (63--67) | 67.1% | -1.5% | -1.8% |

Moderate-duration caregivers show near-zero lifetime employment effects but a small reduction in retirement (-1.5 to -1.8% at 63--67). The leave policy keeps them in the labor force slightly longer.

### 4+ CG years: the leave policy *increases* employment

| Metric (All 30--67) | Baseline | %chg Normal | %chg Full |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employment rate | 65.0% | **+1.0%** | **+1.1%** |
| Share FT | 34.2% | +1.3% | +1.4% |
| Share retired (63--67) | 72.3% | **-9.7%** | **-10.6%** |

Long-duration caregivers are the group most affected by the leave policy, and **the effect is positive**: employment rises by 1%, driven by a **massive 10% reduction in retirement** at 63--67. The mechanism is job retention: agents who would have retired early to provide extended care instead take leave, maintaining their labor market attachment, and return to work or delay retirement.

#### Age-specific patterns for 4+ CG year agents

| Age group | Empl. rate %chg (Normal/Full) |
|---|---|
| 30--39 | +0.4% / +0.7% |
| 40--49 | +0.8% / +1.1% |
| 50--59 | **-3.2% / -3.5%** |
| 55--65 | +2.8% / +2.3% |
| 60--67 | **+15.5% / +15.3%** |

The 60--67 effect for long-duration caregivers is striking: employment jumps by **15.5%**. This is the group with the most to gain from job retention — without the policy, they would exit the labor force permanently to care for elderly parents over 4+ years. The leave policy preserves their employment and pension accumulation.

## 12. Big-picture synthesis

The leave policy operates through two distinct channels with different time horizons:

**Short-run (during caregiving):** Large negative employment effects. Current caregivers reduce work by 15--16%, with the "freed-up" time going to informal care provision. The shift is concentrated in the 50--59 age range where both wages and care demand peak.

**Long-run (lifecycle):** Near-zero or slightly positive net employment effects. The short-run labor reduction is almost exactly offset by:
1. **Delayed retirement** at 60--67, especially for long-duration caregivers.
2. **Job retention** preserving labor market attachment through the caregiving spell.
3. **Experience accumulation** continuing through the leave period, supporting higher pension entitlements (Panel J shows +3.0--3.3% experience at retirement for ever-CG agents).

**Duration gradient:** The policy is essentially irrelevant for short-duration (1 year) caregivers and progressively more important for longer spells. Agents with 4+ caregiving years see the largest benefits: higher lifetime employment and a 10% reduction in early retirement. This is the group the job-retention component of the policy is designed for.

**The retirement channel is the key mechanism** for long-run effects. Without the leave policy, agents forced into multi-year caregiving exit the labor market early and permanently. With it, they take temporary leave and return — preserving both income and pension entitlements.

# Chapter 7 Write-Up: Key Structural Insights

Synthesized findings from the full policy experiments section (all thirteen panels). These insights emerged from integrating the individual panel analyses into a coherent narrative.

## 16. Near-perfect one-for-one substitution between formal and informal care

The increase in informal care under both leave policies is almost exactly matched by a decrease in formal care in percentage-point terms:

| Policy | Δ Informal CG (pp) | Δ Formal care (pp) |
|---|---|---|
| Normal leave | +3.4 pp | −3.4 pp |
| Full leave | +4.2 pp | −4.2 pp |

This holds at the aggregate level and within each education group. The leave policies reallocate the *type* of care provision without changing the total quantity of care demanded. From a fiscal perspective, this near-perfect substitution means the leave policy's formal care savings directly offset part of the leave benefit cost.

## 17. Incentive structure: flat vs earnings-related benefits

A key design difference between the baseline (Pflegegeld) and the leave policies is how transfers vary with labor supply:

- **Pflegegeld**: Flat transfer (~€4,100–4,500/yr), independent of employment. Creates **no marginal incentive** to adjust work behavior.
- **Leave policies**: Transfers are concentrated on agents who **reduce work**. FT caregivers get €0; PT caregivers get €444 (normal) or €7,044 (full); unemployed caregivers get €7,900 (normal) or €16,855 (full).

This incentive reorientation is the **mechanism** behind all the behavioral changes: the leave policies subsidize the act of withdrawing from work to provide care, whereas the Pflegegeld does not. The large employment reductions during active caregiving (Panel E) and the surge in "unemployment" (leave take-up) are direct consequences.

## 18. Temporal reallocation, not net reduction, of labor supply

The most important structural finding: the leave policies **redistribute when agents work, not whether they work**. Lifetime employment of ever-caregivers changes by less than 0.2%, despite 15–16% employment reductions during active caregiving.

The offsetting pattern:
- Ages 30–49: negligible positive effects (+0.1–0.3%)
- Ages 50–59: negative effects (−1.8% to −2.4%) during peak caregiving
- Ages 60–67: positive effects (+4.2%) via delayed retirement

The job retention guarantee is the mechanism that enables this temporal reallocation. Without it, the negative shock at 50–59 would be permanent (agents cannot return to work).

## 19. Intensive vs extensive margin at the first caregiving year

The comparison of normal and full leave at the first caregiving year (Panel K) reveals an important margin distinction:
- Full-time decline: −6.2% (normal) vs −11.1% (full) → full leave causes MUCH larger FT reduction
- Employment rate: −4.2% (normal) vs −4.3% (full) → nearly identical

Implication: the additional generosity of full leave (100% vs 65%) shifts agents along the **intensive margin** (FT → PT) rather than the **extensive margin** (working → not working). This is because the full leave's gap payment for FT→PT transitions is more generous, making part-time caregiving financially viable.

## 20. Duration gradient as the central heterogeneity

The caregiving duration is the single most important moderator of policy effects:

| Duration | Lifetime empl. effect | Retirement effect (63–67) | Empl. effect (60–67) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 year | −0.3% | +0.4% | ~0% |
| 2–3 years | ~0% | −1.5% | ~0% |
| 4+ years | **+1.0%** | **−9.7%** | **+15.5%** |

The **sign reversal** for 4+ year caregivers is remarkable: the policy that reduces employment during caregiving actually *increases* their lifetime employment. The mechanism is entirely through the retirement channel—preventing permanent exit at ages 60–67.

This gradient has clear policy implications: the job retention component is essentially irrelevant for short-duration caregivers and maximally important for long-duration caregivers. Policy design should be tailored to the agents with the longest expected care spells.

## 21. Consumption smoothing through the caregiving episode

Panel H shows that average consumption is virtually unchanged under normal leave and **rises by 0.2%** under full leave for agents under 63. This is despite a 0.2% decline in gross labor income.

The interpretation: the leave benefit successfully replaces the income lost during caregiving, maintaining consumption. The full leave's 100% replacement rate achieves this more effectively than the 65% normal leave. This consumption-smoothing property is welfare-relevant: it suggests the leave policy reduces the utility cost of the caregiving episode by stabilizing the consumption path.

## 22. Job retention as the dominant design element

Comparing normal (65%) and full (100%) leave reveals that:
- **Short-run effects** (CG composition, active CG labor supply) differ substantially between the two policies
- **Long-run effects** (lifetime employment, retirement, experience, pensions) are nearly identical

Since both policies share the job retention guarantee but differ in replacement rate, this implies job retention—not the replacement rate—is the **dominant driver** of long-run outcomes. The replacement rate primarily governs:
1. The intensity of leave take-up (how many stop working entirely)
2. The caregiving composition response (how much informal care replaces formal)
3. The distributional incidence (who benefits from the transfers)

Policy design implication: a less generous replacement rate combined with strong job retention would achieve most of the long-run benefits at lower fiscal cost.

# Who Benefits and Who Loses: Distributional Analysis of Leave vs. Pflegegeld

## 23. The leave policies create winners and losers among caregivers

A critical feature of the leave policies that deserves careful treatment: they **replace** the flat Pflegegeld with an earnings-related benefit conditional on reducing work. This creates distinct groups of winners and losers among caregivers in terms of direct transfers.

### A. Direct transfer comparison by caregiver labor state

| Caregiver labor state | Share (baseline) | Pflegegeld (baseline) | Normal leave | Full leave | Δ Normal | Δ Full |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full-time** | 26.3% | €4,061 | **€0** | **€0** | **−€4,061** | **−€4,061** |
| **Part-time** | 31.7% | €4,235 | €444 | €7,044 | **−€3,791** | +€2,809 |
| **Unemployed (leave)** | 28.8% | €5,186 | €7,900 | €16,855 | +€2,714 | +€11,669 |
| **Retired** | 13.3% | €4,241 | **€0** | **€0** | **−€4,241** | **−€4,241** |

Under **normal leave**, only the 28.8% of caregivers who stop working entirely receive MORE than under Pflegegeld. The remaining **71.2% receive LESS** (FT, PT, and retired caregivers all lose).

Under **full leave**, the picture improves: ~60.5% receive more (unemployed + PT caregivers), but **39.6% still lose** (FT + retired caregivers get nothing).

### B. The average caregiver receives LESS under normal leave

| Metric (All CG, 40–67) | Pflegegeld | Normal leave | Full leave |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avg. benefit per caregiver | **€4,469** | €3,341 | €9,091 |
| Change vs. Pflegegeld | — | **−€1,128** | +€4,622 |

Under normal leave, the average caregiver receives **€1,128 less per year** in direct transfers than under Pflegegeld. The normal leave's advantage over Pflegegeld is NOT in transfer generosity—it is entirely in the **job retention and experience accumulation channels**.

Under full leave, the average caregiver is substantially better off in direct transfers (+€4,622/year). But even here, FT and retired caregivers lose entirely.

### C. Who are the losers? The caregivers who juggle work and care

The most affected losers are **full-time working caregivers** (26.3% of baseline caregivers). These agents combine demanding market work with informal care provision—arguably among the most burdened individuals in the model. Under the leave policies:

- They lose their ~€4,061/year Pflegegeld
- They receive €0 in leave benefits (because the leave benefit compensates only for earnings *losses*, and they have none)
- They get no recognition for their dual burden of working AND caregiving

Under the Pflegegeld, these same agents receive a flat transfer acknowledging their caregiving role regardless of employment. The leave policies effectively **penalize caregivers who continue working full-time**.

**Retired caregivers** (13.3% of baseline, concentrated at ages 60–67 where they are 37.9%) similarly lose their entire Pflegegeld. These are older agents who retired and then provide care to elderly parents—a group with limited ability to "take leave" since they have no job to take leave from.

### D. The education dimension sharpens the distributional concern

The earnings-related structure of the leave benefits compounds the distributional asymmetry along education:

**1. Benefits are wage-proportional → high-edu agents receive larger absolute transfers.** Under both leave policies, the benefit is a function of prior earnings. A high-education unemployed caregiver with a prior FT gross wage of, say, €40,000 receives much more than a low-education counterpart with a prior wage of €18,000. Under Pflegegeld, both received the same flat amount.

**2. Low-education agents are near the lower bound.** The normal leave has a lower bound of €300/month (€3,600/year). A low-education agent whose 65% net wage replacement falls near this bound receives little more than the floor. Since their baseline Pflegegeld was ~€5,186/year (unemployed CG), it is **plausible that some low-education agents receive LESS under normal leave than under Pflegegeld** even when they stop working entirely. This cannot be verified without education-specific benefit data (see Section 24 below).

**3. Agents with no prior job receive nothing.** The leave policies require `job_before_caregiving > 0` (the agent must have had a PT or FT job before caregiving started). Agents with no prior employment receive €0 under leave but would have received Pflegegeld under the baseline. Low-education agents are more likely to have gaps in employment history, making them more likely to be leave-ineligible.

**4. Low-education agents are the inframarginal caregivers.** In the baseline, low-edu agents already provide intensive care at 21.6% (vs 12.6% for high-edu). They are already providing informal care without the leave incentive. The policy primarily induces NEW informal care among high-edu agents (who shift from formal to informal care). The fiscal cost of the policy is thus concentrated on subsidizing high-edu agents' entry into informal care, while low-edu agents—who already bear the burden—see smaller behavioral changes (+6.6% vs +11.4% under normal leave) and receive smaller absolute benefits.

### E. The leave policy's genuine advantage: job retention for long-duration caregivers

The case FOR the leave policies is entirely in the long-run channels:

1. **Job retention** prevents permanent labor market exit for agents who would otherwise retire early to care (Panel M: 4+ year caregivers see −9.7% retirement, +15.5% employment at 60–67)
2. **Experience accumulation** during leave preserves pension entitlements (+3.0% experience years for ever-caregivers)
3. **Delayed retirement** generates higher lifetime earnings and consumption

The Pflegegeld provides NONE of these benefits. An agent who receives Pflegegeld and exits the labor force to provide multi-year care faces job separation, lost experience, and early retirement—all of which the leave policies prevent.

**The fundamental trade-off is therefore:**
- **Pflegegeld**: broadly supports ALL informal caregivers regardless of employment status, but does nothing to prevent the long-run labor market consequences of caregiving
- **Leave policies**: strongly support agents who take leave (especially high earners), but neglect FT-working caregivers and retired caregivers; generate large long-run benefits through job retention

The leave policies are NOT strictly better than Pflegegeld for all caregivers. They are better for the subset who take leave (especially long-duration), and worse for the subset who continue working or are already retired. Whether this is desirable depends on the social objective—and on whether the long-run job retention benefits for long-duration caregivers outweigh the short-run transfer losses for FT-working caregivers.

### F. Is the Pflegegeld better? Not in general—but for some groups, yes.

The Pflegegeld is better for:
- **FT-working caregivers** who combine work and care (26.3% of baseline CG)
- **Retired caregivers** who provide care in old age (13.3%)
- **PT caregivers under normal leave** (31.7%)
- **Agents with no prior job** who are leave-ineligible

The leave policies are better for:
- **Unemployed caregivers** who stop working to provide care (get more under full leave, slightly more under normal leave)
- **Long-duration caregivers** who benefit from job retention (regardless of education)
- **Agents at risk of permanent labor market exit** (the 4+ year caregivers whose retirement drops by 10%)

A policy that COMBINES a flat Pflegegeld-like floor with job retention guarantees—i.e., keeps the unconditional cash benefit while adding the employment protection—would dominate both the pure Pflegegeld and the pure leave policies.
