\section{Estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Method of Simulated Moments}
\label{sec:msm}

We estimate the structural parameters of the life-cycle model using the Method
of Simulated Moments (MSM).  Let $\omega$ denote the vector of preference and
job-finding parameters to be estimated in the second stage, after
pre-estimating the stochastic processes that govern health, mortality, partner
status, wages, job separations, and care demand from auxiliary data.  The MSM
estimator chooses $\omega$ to minimize the weighted distance between moments
computed from the data and the corresponding moments computed from a sample
simulated under the model:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:msm}
  \hat\omega
  \;=\;
  \arg\min_{\omega}\;
  \bigl[\hat\psi - \psi(\omega)\bigr]^{\!\top}
  \,\Sigma\,
  \bigl[\hat\psi - \psi(\omega)\bigr],
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat\psi$ is the vector of empirical moments, $\psi(\omega)$ collects
the simulated counterparts obtained by solving and simulating the life-cycle
model at parameter values~$\omega$, and $\Sigma$ is a diagonal weighting
matrix.  The diagonal elements of~$\Sigma$ are set to the inverse of the
estimated variances of the empirical moments, so that moments measured with
greater precision receive proportionally more weight in the criterion
function.\footnote{Using a diagonal weighting matrix avoids estimating the
full covariance structure of the moments, which is infeasible given the
large moment vector.  The resulting estimator is consistent, and inference
is based on a sandwich-type variance estimator that accounts for the
diagonal approximation.}

The parameter vector~$\omega$ comprises the disutility-of-work parameters
(by employment type, education, health, and caregiving status), the utility
of the caregiving arrangement (informal versus formal care, by education
and health), the job-finding probability parameters, the CRRA and bequest
preference parameters, as well as the scale of taste shocks and the
distribution of unobserved types.  Discount factor, interest rate, wage
equation coefficients, and all stochastic-process parameters are held fixed
at their pre-estimated values.

We solve the model by backward induction on a discretized state space and
simulate 10{,}000 life-cycle histories for each candidate~$\omega$.  The
criterion function~\eqref{eq:msm} is minimized using a derivative-free
optimizer.


\subsection{Moments Used in Estimation}
\label{sec:moments}

Table~\ref{tab:moments_overview} provides an overview of the 969~moments
that enter the MSM criterion.  The moments are organized into five panels.

\textit{Panel~A} contains labor supply shares---retirement, non-employment,
part-time, and full-time---by single year of age from 30 to~70, both pooled
and by education.  These 492~moments pin down the age profile of labor
market participation and its education gradient, forming the backbone of
identification for the disutility-of-work parameters.

\textit{Panel~B} conditions on caregiving status.  We match labor supply
shares of caregivers (all, light, and intensive) in three-year age bins from
40 to~70, both pooled and by education, yielding 360~moments.  These moments
are central to identifying the additional disutility associated with the
double burden of work and caregiving, as they capture how labor supply
behavior shifts when women take on informal care responsibilities.

\textit{Panel~C} targets the caregiving arrangement.  We match the age
profile of informal-care provision (any, light, and intensive) in five-year
age bins from age~40 to~70, separately by education.  Because the German
Socio-Economic Panel (GSOEP) records whether a respondent provides daily
care but does not identify the care recipient, we adjust the observed
caregiving shares using information from the Survey of Health, Ageing and
Retirement in Europe (SHARE).  Specifically, SHARE asks adult daughters
who report providing daily care to identify the recipient, allowing us to
estimate the fraction of informal care directed toward an elderly mother
in each age bin.  We use these age-specific fractions to downweight the
GSOEP caregiving shares so that the resulting moments reflect care
provision to a mother---the relevant margin in our model---rather than
caregiving to any recipient.  We additionally match the share of pure
formal care by care intensity and education, drawn from the GSOEP Innovation
Sample, and the overall share of caregivers with high education.  These
59~moments identify the utility parameters associated with different
caregiving arrangements.

\textit{Panel~D} matches mean household wealth by five-year age bin and
education from the GSOEP, covering ages~30 to~90.  These 24~wealth moments
discipline the consumption--savings margin and the bequest parameters.

\textit{Panel~E} targets dynamic behavior.  We include year-to-year
labor-supply persistence rates (working-to-working and
not-working-to-not-working) by five-year age bin and education, as well as
caregiving persistence rates by education.  These 34~transition moments help
identify the job-finding probabilities and the taste-shock scale parameter
that governs the degree of idiosyncratic preference heterogeneity in the
model.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================================

This section presents the estimated structural parameters and evaluates the
model's ability to replicate the empirical moments.  We first discuss the
preference parameters---the disutility of labor supply, the interaction
between labor supply and children, the additional disutility incurred when
combining work with informal caregiving, the job-finding process, the
utility derived from different caregiving arrangements, and the auxiliary
preference parameters---before turning to the model fit.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Disutility of Labor}
\label{sec:results_disutil}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:disutil_params} reports the estimated disutility parameters.
Panel~A displays the parameters governing the disutility of work and
unemployment in the absence of caregiving responsibilities.

The most striking pattern is the health gradient of full-time employment.
For highly educated women in bad health, the disutility of full-time work
reaches~4.61---roughly seven times the corresponding estimate of~0.63 for
women in good health.  Among the less educated, the gradient is
qualitatively similar but less extreme, rising from~0.21 in good health
to~1.66 in bad health.  Poor health thus constitutes a powerful deterrent
to full-time employment, consistent with the empirical regularity that
health shocks precipitate labor market exit, especially among older workers.

Part-time employment exhibits a distinct pattern.  In good health,
part-time work carries a higher disutility than full-time work for both
education groups (0.67~versus~0.21 for the less educated; 1.73~versus~0.63
for the highly educated).  This ordering reflects the fact that healthy
workers who choose part-time forgo substantial income relative to full-time
employment; the model rationalizes their part-time choice through the
combination of household composition effects and taste heterogeneity
rather than through a low disutility of part-time hours per~se.  In bad
health, the ordering reverses: part-time becomes far less costly than
full-time (0.12~versus~1.66 for the less educated; 0.37~versus~4.61 for
the highly educated), consistent with part-time serving as a
health-compatible mode of labor force participation.

The disutility of unemployment is moderate and somewhat higher for the
highly educated (0.59~versus~0.37), reflecting the stronger labor market
attachment and higher opportunity cost of non-employment in this group.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Interaction of Labor Supply and Children}
\label{sec:results_children}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:disutil_params} reports the interaction between
labor supply and the number of children.  The estimates reveal a clear
work--family trade-off.  For highly educated women, children substantially
increase the disutility of full-time work ($-0.50$ per child), while
reducing the disutility of part-time work ($0.24$).  The pattern for
less-educated women is qualitatively similar but attenuated: full-time
disutility rises modestly with children ($-0.06$), and part-time becomes
slightly more attractive ($0.06$).  These estimates are consistent with
children shifting preferences toward part-time schedules that offer
greater temporal flexibility for child-rearing, an effect that is
particularly pronounced among highly educated women who face a steeper
income--time trade-off.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Disutility of Labor While Caregiving}
\label{sec:results_disutil_care}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Panel~C of Table~\ref{tab:disutil_params} extends the analysis to women
who simultaneously provide informal care to a parent.  The columns
distinguish the intensity of care provision (light versus intensive) and
the caregiver's own health status.

For women in good health, combining full-time work with caregiving entails
substantially larger disutility than full-time work alone.  Among highly
educated women, the disutility of full-time work while providing light
care is~2.55, roughly four times the non-caregiving counterpart of~0.63.
Under intensive care, the figure is~2.43.  For the less educated, the
disutility of full-time work combined with light care~(1.37) far exceeds
the non-caregiving baseline~(0.21).  These estimates quantify the double
burden that empirical studies consistently document: working full-time
while also providing hands-on care to a frail parent is a profoundly
costly combination for women in good health, who constitute the majority
of caregivers.

For women in bad health, the absolute disutility of full-time work is
large in all cases, but the incremental effect of caregiving is less
clear-cut.  The disutility of full-time work without caregiving is already
very high for this group (4.61 for the highly educated, 1.66 for the less
educated), and the corresponding caregiving values are somewhat lower
(3.30 and~0.91 for light care, respectively).  This pattern reflects the
strong selection at work: very few bad-health women choose to combine
full-time work with caregiving, and those who do are a highly selected
group.  The parameters should therefore be interpreted as capturing the
average disutility among the small fraction of women who make this choice,
rather than as implying that caregiving \emph{reduces} the burden of
full-time work.

Part-time work while caregiving likewise carries elevated disutility
relative to the non-caregiving case across most cells, though the
increase is generally less pronounced than for full-time employment.
This is consistent with part-time schedules being more compatible with
caregiving obligations.

The disutility of unemployment while caregiving is modestly higher than
in the non-caregiving case for both education groups (0.64~versus~0.37
for the less educated; 0.63~versus~0.59 for the highly educated).  A
notable feature is that the education gradient in unemployment disutility
nearly vanishes under caregiving: both groups converge to approximately
0.64, suggesting that caregiving responsibilities equalize the
non-employment experience across education levels.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Interaction of Labor Supply and Children While Caregiving}
\label{sec:results_children_care}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Panel~D reports the corresponding children interactions for caregiving
women.  The magnitudes are substantially smaller than in Panel~B,
indicating that children play a more muted role in labor supply decisions
when a caregiving obligation is already present.  For full-time work, the
per-child effect is~$-0.08$ (less educated) and~$-0.10$ (highly educated),
compared to~$-0.06$ and~$-0.50$ in the non-caregiving case.  The
attenuation is consistent with caregiving responsibilities dominating the
time-allocation calculus, leaving less scope for children to differentially
affect the work--non-work margin.

\subsection{Job-Finding Parameters}
\label{sec:results_job_finding}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:job_offer_params} reports the estimated parameters of
the job-finding probability, specified as a logistic function of a
cubic polynomial in age (scaled by~10) and an indicator for high
education.

The negative linear coefficient on age ($-0.19$) implies that the
probability of receiving a job offer declines over the life cycle, an
effect partially offset by the positive quadratic term.  The cubic
term is small and negative, producing a job-finding probability that
decreases gradually in mid-life and more steeply after age~55.  The
positive coefficient on high education ($0.09$) implies a modest
advantage in job-finding for the more educated, consistent with the
higher employability associated with greater human capital.

\subsection{Utility of Caregiving Arrangement}
\label{sec:results_care_utility}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:care_utility_params} presents the estimated utility
associated with the three caregiving arrangements available when a
parent has a care demand: light informal care (provided by the agent),
intensive informal care, and formal care.  The reference category is
``no care''---the case in which another family member provides
informal care.  All parameters are expressed as a function of the
agent's own health and education.

The estimates reveal a pronounced preference for informal care
provision when the caregiver is in good health.  Among the highly
educated, light informal care generates a utility of~1.00, the
largest single entry in the table, reflecting a strong intrinsic
motivation to care personally for a parent with moderate needs.
Less-educated women in good health also derive substantial utility
from intensive informal care~(0.85), suggesting that direct hands-on
involvement with a parent in need is experienced positively when the
caregiver's own health permits.

As the caregiver's own health deteriorates, the utility of informal
care drops sharply.  For highly educated women in bad health, the
utility of light informal care falls to~$-0.01$---effectively
zero---while intensive informal care yields only~0.02.  Among
less-educated women, the decline is less dramatic but still large:
light informal care utility falls from~0.35 to~0.06, and intensive
from~0.85 to~0.09.  The caregiver's own health status thus acts as a
powerful moderator of the willingness to provide hands-on care.

Formal care, by contrast, is consistently associated with negative
utility for healthy caregivers ($-0.34$ for the less educated, $-0.22$
for the highly educated), indicating a revealed preference against
delegating care to professional providers.  When the caregiver is in
bad health, however, formal care utility rises to a small positive
value of approximately~0.04 for both education groups.  The gap
between informal and formal care therefore narrows substantially as
the caregiver's health declines: healthy women strongly prefer
providing care themselves, but as their own capacity diminishes,
formal care becomes a relatively more acceptable alternative.  This
pattern is consistent with a model in which intrinsic motivation and
filial obligation drive informal care provision under favorable health
conditions, while pragmatic considerations---notably the physical
demands of caregiving---shift preferences toward formal arrangements
when the caregiver's own health is compromised.

\subsection{Auxiliary Parameters}
\label{sec:results_auxiliary}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:auxiliary_params} reports the remaining structural
parameters that govern intertemporal preferences, bequest motives,
and unobserved heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Consumption utility.}
The CRRA coefficient is substantially higher for less-educated women
(2.01) than for the highly educated~(0.87), implying greater risk
aversion and a stronger consumption-smoothing motive in the former
group.  This is consistent with less-educated women facing tighter
budget constraints and more volatile income streams, making them
more sensitive to consumption fluctuations.

\paragraph{Bequest utility.}
The bequest motive exhibits a stark education gradient.  Highly
educated women have a bequest scale of~0.34, roughly ten times the
estimate for the less educated~(0.04), indicating a substantially
stronger preference for leaving wealth to heirs.  The bequest
curvature parameter reinforces this pattern: the highly educated
display pronounced curvature~(0.77), while the less educated are near
zero~(0.01).  Together, these estimates imply that bequest
considerations are a first-order driver of savings behavior among
highly educated women---who accumulate more wealth over the life
cycle---but play a negligible role for the less educated.

\paragraph{Preference heterogeneity.}
The taste-shock scale is estimated at~1.00, indicating a substantial
role for unobserved preference heterogeneity in rationalizing the
observed choice patterns.  The share of the reference unobserved type
is approximately~0.73 in both education groups, suggesting that the
distribution of latent types is similar across education levels.
